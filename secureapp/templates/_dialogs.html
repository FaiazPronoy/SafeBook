What happens to what you write

Everything you write here is locked before it is saved. The lock is built from your password, so the only person who can open it is you.

While you are signed in, SafeBook holds the key so it can show you your own things. The moment you sign out, that key is gone. What is left on the server is scrambled text that nobody there can turn back into words, including whoever runs it.

That is also why nobody here can reset your password for you. A new password would not open the old lock. Your recovery code is the second key, and it is the only other way in.

The technical version

Each account has a random 256 bit data key. It is sealed with AES-256-GCM under a wrapping key derived from your password with scrypt, and sealed again under a wrapping key derived from your recovery code. Neither the password nor either wrapping key is stored anywhere.

Every note gets its own content key, sealed under your data key. Sharing a note seals a second copy of that content key under a key the server holds, which is the only reason a shared note can be shown to anyone else.

Private and shared notes

A private note can only be opened with your password. Not by other people, and not by SafeBook itself.

Sharing a note is a real decision rather than a display setting. To show it to other people, SafeBook has to be given a way to open it, and from then on it can. You can take a note back off the feed whenever you like and it goes back to being yours alone.

Switching between the two is instant. Nothing is rewritten and nothing is copied, so a long note is no slower to share than a short one.

The technical version

The note itself never moves. Sharing adds a 60 byte copy of its content key sealed under the server key, and unsharing deletes that copy. The ciphertext is byte for byte the same either way, which is the point of putting keys inside other keys rather than re-encrypting anything.

How a message is sealed

Every account has a pair of matched locks. One half is public, so anyone can lock something for you. The other half is yours, and it is the only thing that opens what they locked.

When you send a message it is locked with the reader's public half. From then on the only person who can open it is the person you sent it to.

A copy is locked for you as well, which is why your sent messages stay readable. Send one anonymously and that copy is never made, so it leaves your hands for good.

The technical version

A fresh AES-256 session key encrypts each message. That session key is wrapped with the recipient's RSA-2048 public key using OAEP, and wrapped a second time with the sender's own public key unless the message is anonymous. The recipient's private key is itself encrypted under their data key, so opening a message needs their password.

What a safety number is for

Every account has a short code of its own, worked out from that account's public lock. It changes if the lock ever changes.

Read yours out to somebody over the phone, or in person, and check it against what SafeBook shows them. If the two match, you are writing to the person you think you are.

If they ever stop matching, stop and ask why. This is the one check software cannot do on your behalf, because the whole point of it is that it does not go through SafeBook.

The technical version

The safety number is a SHA-256 digest of the account's public key, shown in groups of four so it can be read aloud without losing your place. Comparing it out of band is what catches a server handing you its own public key in place of somebody else's.

Your password is also your key

Most sites use your password to check that you are you. Here it does that and one more thing: it builds the key that unlocks your notes and messages.

So changing it is quick and safe. Your notes are not touched at all. Only the small lock that holds your key is rebuilt around the new password.

It also means a weak password is a weak lock, not just a weak login. A long one you can remember beats a short complicated one.

The technical version

An Argon2id hash verifies the password. Separately, scrypt derives a 32 byte wrapping key from the same password, and that key seals your data key. Changing your password rewrites one sealed envelope and leaves every stored note untouched.

Why the recovery code matters

Because your password is what unlocks your things, forgetting it would normally mean losing them. There is nobody here who can open your account for you.

The recovery code is a second lock around the same key. Keep it somewhere real, away from the device you sign in on. It is shown once, and SafeBook keeps no copy it can read.

Anyone holding it can get into your account, so treat it the way you would treat a spare front door key.

Signing in

A wrong password takes the same amount of time whether or not the account exists, and says the same thing either way. Otherwise this page would quietly tell strangers which names are real.

Five wrong attempts in a row pauses that name for fifteen minutes, so nobody can sit there working through a list of guesses.

You can see every attempt on your account, right or wrong, on your account page.

Why something might not open

Every note and message carries a small seal, taken over the locked text at the moment it was saved. Before anything is shown to you, that seal is checked.

If even one character of the stored text has been altered since, the seal will not match and SafeBook will not show you the note. It will not show you a damaged version with a warning next to it either, because a warning you can read past is not really a check.

In normal use you will never see this. It is here so that somebody with access to the database cannot quietly edit what you wrote.

The technical version

Each row carries an HMAC-SHA256 over a length prefixed encoding of all its ciphertext columns and the id of the account that owns it, keyed by a subkey derived from the row key. It is compared in constant time. AES-GCM authenticates each field on its own; the row seal is what stops fields being swapped between rows or a row being moved to another account.
